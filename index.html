Divyanshu Sood

I work out where electrification actually lands. Not how much electricity a country will need, but which transformer districts reach their limit first, which are ageing thermally, and what a planner should do about it.

Senior Energy Systems Researcher, UCD Energy Institute  ·  Module Coordinator, Energy Systems in Buildings II  ·  Data Theme Lead, NexSys

Overview

A mechanical engineer working on energy systems at national scale. I build spatially explicit models of the Irish building stock and the electricity network it loads, train and test the machine learning that runs on them, and teach the thermodynamics and fluid mechanics underneath.

The thread through everything is the gap between a national target and a local consequence. Every heat pump and every car charger connects to a low-voltage network that was designed for neither. Planning runs on national totals, so the question of which transformer fails first, in which settlement, went unanswered. Most of my work is some version of answering it.

The second thread is whether a model deserves to be believed. A model that informs a statutory decision carries a different burden from one that produces a figure for a paper, and much of my recent work is about establishing where that burden is and is not met.

Research

National Energy Digital Twin

A spatially explicit model linking Irish dwellings to the low-voltage transformer districts that serve them, assembled from census small-area statistics, the national building energy rating register, address data and network asset records. A user sets retrofit, heat pump, electric vehicle and rooftop solar uptake and sees which districts breach capacity, which transformers are ageing thermally rather than briefly peaking, which technology caused each breach, and how heat demand density shifts. The output is a ranked reinforcement list rather than a national demand curve. I lead the modelling.

Method. Assigning each dwelling to the transformer minimising distance divided by the square root of rated capacity, with concave-hull catchment boundaries, cut spurious overload artefacts by 71 per cent against nearest-station assignment and eliminated every case above 1000 per cent utilisation.

Whether a learned model can be trusted on a real network

A benchmark for transformer overload screening across 36,997 stations and 128 electrification scenarios, tested under both scenario shift and leave-one-county-out geographic shift.

Finding. Selecting models on accuracy leaves validation error flat while degrading the safety-relevant error, raising missed at-risk stations from 30 to 38 per cent beyond the training range. The apparent ranking between models inverts once the flagged rate is equalised. And two spatial assignments that agree nationally to numerical precision still disagree on 4.5 per cent of overload labels, so part of the uncertainty is irreducible without operator data.

Recovering what is behind a feeder

Network planning needs to know how many heat pumps, electric vehicles and rooftop solar systems sit behind a feeder, and often nobody does. This work recovers adoption fractions from a single year of aggregate hourly load by exploiting each technology's distinct time-frequency signature, comparing five estimators from interpretable dictionaries to neural networks.

Cities and towns do not face the same problem

A glide-path study across two contrasting feeders found that a rural town network on 50 kVA pole-mounted transformers reaches its limits at far lower adoption than a Dublin suburb on 400 kVA units, so a uniform national target produces very different stress depending on where you live.

Teaching

I coordinate Energy Systems in Buildings II, a core postgraduate module in mechanical and materials engineering, and I treat changes to it as something to test rather than assert.

The module runs on a deliberate half-and-half split between lectures and project work. Students choose a real building and location, obtain local climate data, model it, and defend a retrofit proposal in a presentation where their assumptions are questioned. It builds on thermodynamics, heat transfer, fluid dynamics and psychrometrics, extending into load calculation, HVAC design and the thermal performance of building elements.

I moved the module onto IES VE, an industry-standard platform, so that the skills transfer to professional practice and students spend their effort on analysis rather than on fighting the software. I then evaluated the design and its dual-assessment structure and published the findings at the European Conference on Computing in Construction.
